## PAGE 1 — Maps
### (Battery Drop-Off Access by County + Community Type side by side)

**Title text box (top of page):**
Michigan Battery Access Gap — Income & Geography

**Callout text box (below or between maps):**
The map on the left shows drop-off site density by county. The map on the right shows community type. The pattern is consistent: rural counties in the north and Upper Peninsula have the least access. Urban and suburban counties in the south have the most. The gap is not random — it follows both income and geography.

## PAGE 2 — Income vs Access
### (Raw sites by quartile + Per capita sites by quartile side by side)

**Title text box (top of page):**
The 21x Gap — and Why Raw Numbers Are Misleading

**Callout text box (between the two charts):**
The left chart shows raw drop-off sites by income quartile. Q2 appears higher than Q3 — that is Wayne County inflating the number. Wayne County has 23 sites, but with 1.7 million residents, that is only 1.33 sites per 100,000 people — one of the lowest rates in the state. The right chart corrects for population. Q2 drops back below Q3 where it belongs. The lowest income counties average 0.86 sites per 100k residents. The highest average 3.00. The access gap is real and consistent across both measures.

## PAGE 3 — Target Counties
### (Battery Desert Counties table)

**Title text box (top of page):**
38 Counties. Zero Infrastructure.

**Callout text box (beside or below table):**
These 38 counties have no battery drop-off sites. They are home to some of Michigan's lowest median household incomes and are disproportionately rural. The residents here are not choosing to throw batteries in the trash — they have no alternative. Food pantries and libraries in these counties are the recommended entry points for site placement. They are trusted, accessible, and already visited by the residents most underserved by the current system.

## PAGE 4 — County Analysis
### (Scatter plot — Income vs Battery Access)

**Title text box (top of page):**
Income Predicts Access — But Not Perfectly

**Callout text box (beside scatter plot):**
Each dot represents one Michigan county. The horizontal axis is median household income. The vertical axis is battery drop-off sites per 100,000 residents. The cluster of dots at zero along the bottom are the 38 battery desert counties — nearly all of them sit on the left side of the chart, in the lowest income ranges. The trend line confirms the correlation: as income increases, access increases. But the relationship is not clean. Some low-income counties outperform expectations. Some middle-income counties have almost nothing. The story is structural, not absolute — and it points to the same conclusion: where retail infrastructure doesn't exist, neither does recycling access.
